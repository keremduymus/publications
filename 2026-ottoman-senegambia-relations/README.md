- ## Abstract

This study reconstructs Ottoman–Senegambian relations from the fifteenth-century historical context to the mid-nineteenth-century direct relations, challenging the assumption that there was no engagement between the Islamic states in the Senegambian region and the Ottoman Empire. Employing a global Afro-Ottoman perspective and integrating Ottoman, African, and European archives, alongside newspapers and merchant correspondence, the analysis reveals three phases and backgrounds of interaction. First, Ottoman corsairs were present in the Atlantic waters from the late fifteenth century to the mid-eighteenth century yet deliberately avoided West African coasts for various reasons. Second, from the early eighteenth century onward, Fulbe-led jihad movements established Islamic states in Senegambia (Bundu, Fuuta Jaloo, Fuuta Tooro), integrating the region into trans-regional Islamic networks centred in Mecca and Istanbul. Third, following the Tanzimat reforms in the 1830s and the Berlin Conference in 1884–1885, the Ottoman Empire pursued diplomatic, commercial, and intelligence-gathering outreach with the Senegambian region, establishing consular offices in Dakar while maintaining spiritual claims over the region’s Muslim communities. This history demonstrates the global interplay of Ottoman and African agencies connecting two distant regions previously treated as separate.
